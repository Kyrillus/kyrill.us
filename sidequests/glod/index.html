Robotics x Art (ft. Glod)

In the summer of 2026 the artist Marcin Glod approached Alex, who runs HOIV (Home of Innovation). Glod had a showroom for two months for his new collection, a glass case in the passage of the U1 station at Karlsplatz in Vienna. He wanted something in it that mixed art with tech. That's how my friend Josef Nasim and I ended up building this.

We eventually decided to build a robot arm that holds a sign and shows it to you when you smile.

There's a screen in the middle of the case with a webcam on it. When nobody is around it says “Come closer!” When it sees a face it says “Smile more!” and draws a pink ring that fills up as you smile. If you smile enough, the screen says “I love your smile!”, a small robot arm on a column next to it turns its sign around so you can read “I like your smile!”, the big pinwheel turns and the clouds overhead start to move.

Building it

The smile detector and the robot arm started out as two separate experiments. We only connected them the night we set it up. By half past four in the morning we were inside the case, sitting on the artificial grass with a laptop while Glod rolled in the screen and put up the pinwheel. By a quarter to ten it was running.

How it works

A Raspberry Pi looks at the webcam picture, finds the faces in it and asks an off-the-shelf model how much each one is smiling. If several people are standing there, the biggest smile counts. The camera picture never appears on the screen.

The arm is an SO-101, an open-source design you can print yourself. It starts to rise as soon as you start smiling and stops a few times on the way up. At 90 percent it goes all the way, turns the sign, holds it up for five seconds and sinks back down.

What broke

It had to run for two months in a subway station with neither of us there. Mostly it did.

The Raspberry Pi wasn't getting quite enough power. Every so often the voltage dipped, the USB ports reset and whatever was plugged into them vanished for a moment. The first time, in the first week, the part of the program that moves the arm died while the rest kept running. The screen got stuck on “I love your smile!” and said that to everyone who walked past, smiling or not, until we noticed.

So we wrote a watchdog, a second small program that restarts the first one when it stops answering.

Then, on a Thursday afternoon in late June, the camera dropped out and a restart couldn't bring it back. Nobody could set it off for almost four days, until we logged in on Monday morning and reset the USB ports by hand. We taught the watchdog to do that too. The next time the camera vanished it was back in three seconds.

At about the same moment, though, five of the arm's six motors stopped answering and the program won't start without the arm. The watchdog restarted it every two and a half minutes for the next 57 hours, 1,365 times in all. It crashed every time. In the end one of us went to the station and pushed the cables back in.

After that it ran every day for more than three weeks. Then the camera disappeared again and this time resetting the ports didn't bring it back. It was still down when we collected the logs a week later.
